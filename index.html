Module 01

What is 3D printing?

  • GoalTell additive manufacturing apart from carving material away.
  • GoalPlace a few true events on a short history line.
  • GoalName real uses in medicine, space, food, and buildings, without the science-fiction extras.

A 3D printer is like a very careful hot-glue robot. It draws a shape, steps up a tiny bit, and draws again. Stack enough drawings and you can hold the object.

3D printing is additive manufacturing. A digital model is sliced into layers and a machine adds material only where the part needs it. Subtractive work, like milling or carving, starts with a block and cuts the scrap away.

Custom explainer slot. Drop in the mp4 and this poster steps aside.

On-screen intro

Adding, not carving

A printer builds a solid by stacking thin layers of plastic. The computer decides the path. The machine only melts and moves.

Interactive: build or carve

Additive: the shape grows as layers stack. You only melt the plastic the part needs, plus a little skirt or support.

Project: layer cookie

Stack three cardboard circles, each smaller than the one under it. That is a layer cake model of a dome. Then design the same idea as a keychain tag in Tinkercad: your initials, 4 mm thick, with a 5 mm hole.

Project: waste compare

Sketch a 40 mm gear. Estimate the block you would carve (include the grip the tool needs) and the plastic a printer would add. Write one sentence on which process throws away more material for a one-off part, and when carving still wins.

Quick check · grades 5–6

3D printing is mostly…

Additive means the part grows from nothing, layer by layer.

Which one is subtractive?

Whittling removes material. That is subtractive.

A true space story is…

The file traveled as data. The printer on the station made the object.

Your school printer can reliably…

Desktop FDM is great at small plastic parts. Houses need giant concrete printers and a full building crew.

Quick check · grades 7–8

Chuck Hull’s 1984 patent is for…

SLA came first commercially. FDM, the melted-filament process in most school printers, is Scott Crump’s later invention.

Why did cheap desktop printers spread after about 2009?

The machines got easier to copy when core patents lapsed and the RepRap community published plans.

A careful claim about medicine is…

Models and guides are in use. Printing living replacement organs is not a classroom result.

Strength of an FDM part depends on…

Layers can split. A clip printed on its side can be stronger than the same clip printed standing up.

Module 02

How a printer works

  • GoalName the nozzle, hotend, extruder, bed, gantry, and filament.
  • GoalExplain how a part grows one layer at a time.
  • GoalPoint to each part on a diagram.

Think of a robot holding a tiny hot glue gun. The glue stick is the filament. The robot draws, lifts a hair, and draws again.

Most school machines are FDM: fused deposition modeling. A cold extruder pushes solid filament into a hotend. The nozzle lays a road of melt. The gantry steps up by the layer height, often 0.12–0.28 mm, and the next road welds on.

On-screen intro

Melt, move, stack

Filament goes in solid, comes out as a thin hot line, and freezes into the layer under it.

Pick a part of the printer.

Project: trace a layer

On graph paper, draw your initials as a path the nozzle could follow without lifting. Then draw the same letters one square smaller on top. Two layers. That is the whole idea.

Project: layer math

A 12 mm tall tag at 0.20 mm layers needs 12 / 0.20 = 60 layers. At 0.12 mm it needs 100 layers. Write which one shows less stair-step on a curve, and why the finer one takes longer.

Quick check · grades 5–6

The nozzle is…

The nozzle is the small hot tip. The spool is the filament supply.

The extruder…

Pushing is the extruder’s job. The bed is the table.

A part gets taller because…

FDM is layer by layer. Nothing appears fully formed.

While it is printing, the nozzle is…

PLA nozzles are often near 200°C. That is far hotter than cocoa.

Quick check · grades 7–8

Which part melts the plastic?

The extruder only drives the filament. Melting happens in the hotend.

A 0.20 mm layer height means…

Layer height is the vertical step. Nozzle diameter is the width of the hole, often 0.4 mm.

Why do layers stick?

FDM bonds by heat and pressure, not by a separate glue in the filament.

The gantry is…

Gantry means the moving frame: belts, rails, or screws in X, Y, and Z.

Module 03

Safety and the maker space

  • GoalKeep hands off hot parts and moving belts.
  • GoalSay why ventilation and distance matter.
  • GoalKnow which tools only a teacher uses.

The printer is not a toy oven. The nozzle and sometimes the bed are hot, the belts can pinch, and the plastic smell means you should not hover over the machine.

FDM gives off ultrafine particles and volatile gases. NIOSH and school-health guidance say to ventilate, avoid standing over a running printer, use the lowest temperature that still prints well, and prefer PLA over higher-temperature materials such as ABS in classrooms. A long print is a fire risk if nobody is around and the building air system is off.

Quick check · grades 5–6

The nozzle while printing is…

Do not touch the nozzle, hotend, or a hot bed.

A water bottle belongs…

Drinks spill into electronics and onto hot parts. Eat somewhere else.

Who removes a stuck print with a scraper?

Scrapers cut. The point faces away from hands and bodies.

If you smell hot plastic, you should…

Distance is part of the protection. Do not hover.

Quick check · grades 7–8

Why is PLA the school default instead of ABS?

PLA is easier and lower temperature. It still needs ventilation, and school prints are not food-safe.

A dust mask from art class…

School guidance treats enclosure, exhaust, and not hovering as the real controls.

An overnight print with the air system off is…

Heaters can fail. Someone responsible has to be able to stop the machine, and the room still needs air movement.

Lowest useful nozzle temperature…

Hotter often means more particles and gases. Stay inside the filament label.

Module 04

Materials

  • GoalCompare PLA, PETG, TPU, and ABS.
  • GoalExplain why this makerspace starts with PLA.
  • GoalPick a material for a job, including “do not print that here.”

Filament is the colorful spaghetti. PLA is the one we use. It prints at a lower temperature, sticks more easily, and comes in almost any color. It can still get soft in a hot car.

These are all thermoplastics. PLA (polylactic acid) is made from plant starch and softens near 60°C. PETG is tougher and a bit more heat resistant, and it strings. TPU is elastic. ABS is strong and heat tolerant, warps, and releases styrene; it is not the classroom default. Typical nozzle ranges, always overridden by the spool label: PLA about 190–220°C, PETG about 230–250°C, TPU about 220–240°C printed slowly, ABS about 230–260°C with a hot bed and an enclosure.

Quick check · grades 5–6

Our school filament is…

PLA is the default. ABS stays off the class printer.

TPU is the one that…

TPU is flexible. A name tag wants stiff PLA.

A PLA tag in a hot car may…

PLA’s glass transition is near 60°C. A closed car gets hotter than that.

School prints and snacks…

Grooves hold bacteria, and “plant-based” is not a food-safety stamp.

Quick check · grades 7–8

PETG compared with PLA is usually…

PETG is a functional step up. It still needs ventilation, and the spool label sets the temperature.

A spool that feels brittle and prints with pops may be…

PETG and TPU absorb water. Drying is a teacher-run step, not a student experiment with an oven.

The gram estimate on this site uses…

PLA is a bit denser than water. The slicer is still the real measurement.

ABS in this room…

ABS can be the right industrial choice in a ventilated enclosure. It is the wrong default beside students.

Module 05

Designing in free CAD

  • GoalPick a free CAD tool that fits your grade.
  • GoalBuild a solid from simple shapes and a hole.
  • GoalKnow that an STL or 3MF is what a slicer wants.

Grades 5–6 start in Tinkercad. You drag boxes, cylinders, and roofs. A hole shape subtracts. Group them and you have a solid. BlocksCAD is the same idea with snap-together code blocks if you like Scratch.

Stay in Tinkercad until holes, alignment, and millimeters feel normal. Then move to a parametric modeler: Autodesk Fusion (free education access) or Onshape Education. FreeCAD is fully open source. SketchUp Free (now on Trimble) is a lighter web modeler. Parametric means you can change a number, like “pin clearance 0.3 mm,” and the part updates.

Which tool fits

Grades 5–6

Best start: Tinkercad. Next: BlocksCAD. Design in millimeters. Export STL.

Grades 7–8

Best start: Tinkercad, then Fusion or Onshape. Use sketches, extrudes, and assemblies. FreeCAD if you want open source offline.

Module 06

Free 3D model libraries

  • GoalFind a model on a real library site.
  • GoalRead a Creative Commons license before you download.
  • GoalCredit the maker every time.

Libraries to know: Printables, Thingiverse, MyMiniFactory, Thangs, NASA 3D Resources, Smithsonian 3D, and the free filter on Cults. A download button is not the same as permission to sell.

If the license says NonCommercial, you may print it for class and for a gift. You may not sell it at the school fair. If it says NoDerivatives, do not change it. Always write the maker’s name.

Copyright exists as soon as someone makes a model. A Creative Commons license is how they give permission in advance. CC BY needs credit. CC BY-SA needs credit and the same license on your remix. CC BY-NC blocks sales. CC BY-ND blocks changes. CC0 is a public-domain dedication. NASA and Smithsonian files can have their own terms on the item page. Read that page.

Module 07

Slicing and printing

  • GoalSay what a slicer does.
  • GoalChange layer height, infill, and supports on purpose.
  • GoalEstimate time and grams, then trust the slicer preview more.

A slicer cuts the model into layers and writes the path. Free ones: PrusaSlicer, UltiMaker Cura, Bambu Studio, and OrcaSlicer. Thicker layers are faster and more visible. Infill is the pattern inside. Supports hold up steep overhangs.

The slicer turns a solid into G-code: temperatures, paths, and speeds. Layer height is usually 25–75% of the nozzle diameter. More perimeters often add more strength than a jump from 15% to 40% infill. Supports cost time and leave scars. The calculator below is the same classroom model used on the take-home lab: shell share plus infill, PLA at 1.24 g/cm³, about 12 mm³/s at 0.20 mm layers. It is not a slicer.

A slicer…

The slicer is software. The printer does the heating.

Infill is…

Infill is hidden structure. 15% is plenty for many tags.

Supports are for…

Quick check · grades 7–8

Height changes the count of layers more than the volume. Time goes up.

G-code is…

The slicer writes G-code. You rarely edit it by hand in this class.

For many clips, strength comes first from…

Walls carry a lot of the load. 100% infill is slow and can warp.

The 12 mm³/s number in the estimator…

Real flow depends on the nozzle, material, and speed limits.

Module 08

Troubleshooting

  • GoalRecognize stringing, warping, spaghetti, and a bad first layer.
  • GoalName the first fix a teacher would try.
  • GoalChange one thing at a time.

Prints fail in ways you can see. Hairs between parts are stringing. Lifted corners are warping. A tangle of plastic where the part used to be is spaghetti. A messy first layer means the nozzle height is wrong. Tell a teacher. Do not turn knobs alone.

Diagnose, then change one setting. Stringing: dry filament, then temperature and retraction inside the spool’s range. Warping: clean bed, brim, no drafts; ABS is worse than PLA. Spaghetti: the part released or an overhang had no support. First layer: too close grinds the bed, too far leaves round loose lines. Prusa’s first-layer guide is a solid reference for the picture, even on other machines.

More first-layer area, especially at corners, fights lift.

Elephant’s foot (a squashed bottom edge) often means…

Too far looks like separate lines. Too close smooshes plastic outward.

Module 09

3D printing for VEX Robotics

  • GoalTell VEX IQ and VEX V5 hole patterns apart using official sizes.
  • GoalDesign a practice part around that grid, with clearance you can test.
  • GoalKeep competition parts and practice parts in two different piles.

VEX IQ is the system many grades 5–6 teams build with: plastic beams, connector pins, and a snap fit. Official pitch is 12.70 mm (0.5 in). The standard snap hole is 4.20 mm across and about 4.57 mm deep. Square shaft holes are 3.20 mm. Those numbers are from VEX’s “Important VEX IQ Beam Dimensions” drawing dated 2014-03-26, and the pitch is repeated in the VEX Library article on the plastic construction system.

VEX V5 structure is metal. Product pages for plates and C-channels say holes are on 0.500 in increments and the opening is 0.182 in square (VEX calls that the standard hole). The kit’s structural screws are #8-32, listed on the V5 Classroom Starter Kit and in VEX structure lessons. A round printed hole is not the same shape as that square. Start a test coupon around 4.8–5.2 mm and adjust. That range is a classroom suggestion, not a VEX specification.

Practice only Check this season

Custom prints are for practice robots, pits, cable organization off the field, jigs, and prototypes. Whether any printed piece may go on a competition robot is decided by this season’s game manual and the Q&A on RobotEvents, not by this course.

Checked for this page, and already different from older seasons: VIQRC 2026–2027 Level Up, Game Manual 2.0, lists 3D printed parts as prohibited for any purpose, including license plates, license plate holders, and non-functional decorations. It still allows 3D-printed controller attachments that do not physically or electrically modify the controller. V5RC 2026–2027 Override, version 0.1.2 (14 May 2026), lists 3D printed robot parts as prohibited for any purpose, including non-functional decorations and license plates. It says 3D printed controller attachments, alignment tools, and other tools that do not go on the robot or into the field during a match are not robot parts. An older Q&A (VRC 2022–2023) treated some 3D printed decorations differently. That is why “I saw it online” is not a rule. Decorations can be legal while 3D printed parts are still banned. Read the manual your event is using. This course is not a head referee.

Module 10

From idea to business

  • GoalWalk a design-thinking loop: people, problem, ideas, prototype, test.
  • GoalCompare making-and-selling with licensing.
  • GoalPrice a small product without pretending this is legal advice.

Riley, a fictional Jordan Glen student, noticed headphones falling off desks. Riley printed a hook, tested it, and sold six at the school fair for $3 with a teacher’s help. A shop later asked to make the hook. That second path is licensing: they make it, Riley’s family might get a small payment from each sale. Nothing here is a real company offer.

Same story, more numbers. Filament, failed prints, a paper tag, and fair fees are costs. Profit is what remains after those, not the sticker price. A license might pay a royalty, a percent or a few cents per item, negotiated by adults. Patents, trademarks, and copyright are different tools. This page is a classroom picture, not legal advice. A parent or the school decides before anyone signs an NDA or talks to a company.

Catapult parts

Module 1.5 mm gears: pinion 12 teeth, gear 36 teeth, center distance 36 mm, ratio 3. Moving holes are 0.3 mm over a 5.0 mm axle (5.3 mm). The crank uses a 0.2 mm clearance and a flat so it turns the axle instead of spinning free. The stop peg picks one of three holes to change the launch. Tie this to the gear slider in the VEX module: driven ÷ driver.

Teacher prep

Budget the class totals above as extrusion time, then add first-layer checks. One printer cannot finish 20 robots in a single class period. Queue bodies or bases overnight only under the school’s supervised-printer rule. Buy a little extra PLA for holes that need a second try. Pom-poms and size-32 or smaller rubber bands are shared supplies, not printed parts. Grades 7–8 should calculate 36/12 before they wind the arm. Grades 5–6 personalize in Tinkercad rather than editing the mesh by hand.
