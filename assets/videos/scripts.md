## 1. What Is 3D Printing? (`what-is-3d-printing`)

Imagine building something from nothing, one thin layer at a time! That's 3D printing, also called additive manufacturing. A printer melts plastic and stacks it layer by layer until a whole object appears. It's not just for toys. Doctors print custom prosthetic hands. Space engineers print rocket parts. And builders can even print the walls of real houses! The big idea is simple: if you can imagine it and design it, you can make it. Let's learn how!

## 2. How a 3D Printer Works (`how-a-printer-works`)

Let's meet the parts of an FDM 3D printer! Plastic string called filament starts on a spool. The extruder pulls it in and pushes it into the hot nozzle, where it melts. The gantry moves the nozzle left, right, and up, drawing each layer onto the build plate. When one layer is done, the nozzle rises a tiny bit and draws the next. Layer after layer, a 3D object grows. Spool, extruder, nozzle, plate, gantry: a team working together!

## 3. Stay Safe in the Maker Space (`safety`)

Great makers are safe makers! The nozzle gets very hot, and so does the build plate, so never touch them while a printer is running or cooling. Always have an adult nearby. Print in a room with fresh air and good ventilation. Keep your maker space tidy: put tools away, keep your hair and sleeves back, and keep walkways clear. Wait for the printer to cool, then ask an adult to help remove your print. Safety first, then creativity!

## 4. Choosing Your Material (`materials`)

Different jobs need different filaments! PLA is the best choice for school. It's easy to print, made from plants, and low-smell. PETG is tougher and a bit flexible, great for parts that take a beating. TPU is soft and bendy like rubber, perfect for phone cases and grippy feet. ABS is very strong and heat-resistant, but it needs special ventilation, so it's mostly for experts. Start with PLA, and then explore the rest!

## 5. Design Your Own in CAD (`cad-design`)

Every print starts as an idea on a screen! CAD means computer-aided design, and there's free software to try. Tinkercad is perfect for beginners, Fusion is great for older students, and Onshape runs right in your browser. Start with simple shapes like boxes, cylinders, and spheres. Snap them together to build something new. Use hole shapes to cut openings, and extrude to pull a flat sketch up into a solid. Save your design, and get ready to print!

## 6. Find Free 3D Models (`model-libraries`)

You don't always have to start from scratch! Free libraries are packed with ready-to-print models. Printables and Thingiverse have millions of designs shared by makers around the world. NASA 3D Resources lets you print real spacecraft and planets. But remember, designers own their work. Check the Creative Commons license. It tells you if you can remix it, share it, or use it in a class project. Always give credit to the creator. That's how great makers respect each other!

## 7. Slicing: Model to Layers (`slicing`)

A printer can't read a 3D model directly. It needs a slicer! Slicer software cuts your model into hundreds of thin layers and writes instructions called G-code. You can choose settings. Layer height sets how thick each layer is: thinner looks smoother but takes longer. Infill is the pattern inside: more infill makes it stronger. And supports are little scaffolds that hold up overhangs, which you snap off afterward. Slice, check the preview, then print!

## 8. Fix Common Print Problems (`troubleshooting`)

Even experts have prints that go wrong, and that's okay! Warping is when corners curl up. Fix it by cleaning the plate and using a good first layer. Stringing leaves thin hairs of plastic. Try drying your filament and adjusting your temperature. Spaghetti happens when the print comes loose and turns into a tangle. Stop it quickly and start again. A bad first layer? Re-level the plate and make sure the nozzle is the right distance. Troubleshooting is how makers get better!

## 9. From Idea to Business (`business`)

Your invention could be a real business! First, make a prototype and test it. Next, figure out your cost: filament, printing time, and packaging. Then set a price that covers your costs and leaves some profit. Now choose your path. You can make and sell it yourself, or license your design to a company. With licensing, the company makes and sells it, and pays you a small percentage called a royalty. Either way, great ideas start with a maker like you!

## 10. Your Capstone Invention (`capstone`)

It's time for your big project! Start by finding a real problem, then design a solution in CAD. Print your first version, and test it honestly. What works? What breaks? Improve your design, and print again. Great inventors repeat this loop many times. Finally, present your invention to the class. Show your sketches, your early versions, and your final design, and tell the story of how you solved the problem. You're an inventor now!

## 11. 3D Printing for VEX Robotics (`vex-robotics`)

Want a faster, smarter robot? Print your own custom parts! Make sensor mounts, cable guides, and intakes that fit your exact design. VEX IQ and V5 parts use a half inch hole pattern, so design your holes and spacing to line up perfectly. For strong, tough parts, print in PETG. And before you build, always check this season's game manual, because the rules say what you can use in competition. Design smart, print strong, and compete with confidence!

## 12. Your Take-Home Project (`take-home-project`)

Time to make something to keep! Grades five and six build the Desk Buddy Bot, a snap-together pen-holder robot with a swivel head and a name plate. Grades seven and eight build the Crank-Gear Mini Catapult that tosses a pom-pom! The steps are the same. Design it, print the parts, assemble it, personalize it with your own style, test it, and take it home. Show your family what you made!
